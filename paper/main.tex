\begin{abstract}
\textsc{FaithLM} evaluates whether a natural-language explanation is faithful by
negating it and measuring how much the target model's prediction shifts. We
reproduce its COPA setting with the paper's own models and obtain a fidelity of
$0.872$ against the $\sim\!0.85$ reported, then ask what that number measures.
Two independently designed controls, absent from the original work, give the
same answer: a hint that is \emph{irrelevant} to the question already flips the
target on $55.4\%$ of instances, and a hint written for a \emph{different}
question flips it on $14.0\%$, so a large share of the reported score reflects
how readily the target follows any hint rather than what the explanation
contains. We further show that the released implementation reads predictions by
regular-expression matching over generated text, discarding $45$--$47\%$ of
Vietnamese predictions as unparseable. Replacing that step with the probability
reading the paper itself specifies raises the flip rate from $0.374$ to $0.509$
($p=0.00011$), and a control isolates the parser, not the continuous signal, as
the source of the gain. On Vietnamese XCOPA the choice of target model moves
accuracy from $11.8\%$ to $92.5\%$, which dominates every effect the metric
reports.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Explanations produced by large language models are fluent but not necessarily
\emph{faithful}: they need not describe the computation that produced the
answer \citep{jacovi2020faithfully,turpin2023unfaithful}. \textsc{FaithLM}
\citep{chuang2026faithlm} proposes to measure faithfulness by intervention.
Given an explanation $E_{\text{NL}}$ of a prediction, an explainer LLM writes a
\emph{contrary hint} $\neg E_{\text{NL}}$ asserting the opposite, and fidelity
is the resulting shift in the target model's output,
\begin{equation}
  S_E := f(X) - f(X \mid \neg E_{\text{NL}}).
  \label{eq:se}
\end{equation}
A large shift is read as evidence that the explanation carried
decision-relevant content.

We reproduce this measurement and then interrogate it. Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
\item \textbf{Reproduction.} Using the paper's own target and explainer, the
  COPA fidelity we obtain matches the published curve
  (\S\ref{sec:repro}).
\item \textbf{The score is not specific to the explanation.} Two controls
  designed independently by different authors of this report---an off-topic hint
  and a hint written for another question---agree that a substantial fraction of
  the score survives when the explanation's content is removed
  (\S\ref{sec:control}).
\item \textbf{The reader, not the metric, breaks under language transfer.} The
  released code resolves predictions with a regular expression over generated
  text. Reading probabilities instead---as \S3.1 of the original specifies---
  recovers a significantly larger effect (\S\ref{sec:parser}).
\item \textbf{A per-language XCOPA evaluation.} The original transfers COPA to
  XCOPA without naming a language; we report Vietnamese explicitly and show the
  target model dominates (\S\ref{sec:vi}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

Post-hoc explanation of black-box LLMs is usually evaluated either by
\emph{simulatability}---can a model reproduce predictions from the
explanation \citep{madsen2024selfexp}---or by perturbing the input and observing
the output. \citet{jacovi2020faithfully} argue these test different properties
and should not be conflated. Chain-of-thought text is a tempting proxy for
reasoning but is demonstrably unfaithful in controlled settings
\citep{turpin2023unfaithful,lanham2023measuring}. \textsc{FaithLM} sidesteps
token-level deletion, which is ill-defined when the decision-relevant content is
latent rather than present in the input, by intervening at the level of meaning.
Our concern is not with that move but with the absence of a null condition
against which the resulting shift is compared.

\section{Dataset}

\paragraph{Balanced COPA.} \citet{roemmele2011copa} pose binary causal
commonsense questions; \citet{kavumba2019balanced} add mirrored instances to
suppress lexical shortcuts. We use the $500$-item test split.

\paragraph{XCOPA-vi.} \citet{ponti2020xcopa} translate COPA into $11$ languages
with professional translators. Crucially, the Vietnamese test split contains
\emph{the same $500$ items} as the English split, verified item-by-item, so the
two form a parallel corpus in which language is the only variable. This makes
the cross-lingual comparison controlled rather than merely suggestive. Neither
dataset carries gold explanations, which is why we cannot report the original's
second metric (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Method}

\paragraph{The measurement.} For each instance the target answers the question;
an explainer proposes $E_{\text{NL}}$ from the (question, answer) pair alone;
the explainer then writes $\neg E_{\text{NL}}$; the target answers again with
$\neg E_{\text{NL}}$ prepended as a hint. Fidelity is
Eq.~\ref{eq:se}. An optimisation loop rewrites the explanation to maximise this
score, terminating early once a non-zero score appears.

\paragraph{Control A: off-topic hint.} We score a third prompt, identical in
shape, whose hint is unrelated to the question (``The weather forecast mentions
scattered clouds tomorrow afternoon.''). Any flip here cannot come from the
explanation's content.

\paragraph{Control B: mismatched hint.} A stricter variant injects the contrary
hint written for a \emph{different} instance. It matches the register, length
and form of a real hint and differs only in which question it belongs to, so it
cannot be dismissed as trivially ignorable.

\paragraph{Score modes.} The released code computes
$|\Delta\text{accuracy}|$ over an argmax answer parsed out of generated text.
We add two alternatives that keep Eq.~\ref{eq:se} but change how $f$ is read:
\textsc{prob\_accuracy}, which takes the argmax over choice probabilities, and
\textsc{logprob}, which uses the continuous probability shift.

\section{Experimental Setup}

We follow Table~2 of the original for COPA: $20$ optimisation steps, target
temperature $0.7$, explainer temperature $0.9$, top-$p$ $0.9$, $500$ instances.
Target \texttt{microsoft/phi-2} and explainer \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo} are the
paper's own choices \citep{javaheripi2023phi2}. Because both of the original's
explainers have since been retired, a separate axis substitutes four current
explainers with everything else fixed. Additional targets
(Qwen3.5-4B, DeepSeek-v4-flash) probe the Vietnamese setting. All runs, raw
per-instance outputs and the code are released with this report.

Two implementation details of the released code affect any reproduction. First,
\texttt{do\_sample} is never set, so \texttt{transformers} silently ignores the
temperature the paper specifies and decodes greedily; we verified that both
regimes give the same result, but a reproduction that follows Table~2 is not
running the released configuration. Second, the early-stopping condition is only
tested every fifth iteration, so $16.6\%$ of executed iterations occur after the
stopping criterion is already satisfied.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{The reproduction succeeds}
\label{sec:repro}

On Balanced COPA with Phi-2 and GPT-3.5, fidelity reaches $0.872$ over $500$
instances against the $\sim\!0.85$ the original reports for COPA. Substituting a
modern explainer under the same protocol gives $0.860$. The measurement is
reproducible.

\subsection{But the score is largely non-specific}
\label{sec:control}

The contrary hint flipped \emph{every} parseable prediction: all $354$ correct
answers and all $34$ incorrect ones score exactly $1.0$. A metric at its ceiling
cannot rank explanations, and saturation is equally consistent with a target
that simply follows hints.

Table~\ref{tab:controlA} shows Control A. Control B, run independently on a
different target and explainer, agrees: an own-question hint flips $0.370$ of
instances against $0.140$ for a hint belonging to another question
($p<0.0001$, $n=200$). The two controls differ in design, target and explainer
yet land in the same place---baselines of $0.122$ and $0.140$, corrected effects
of $+0.214$ and $+0.230$. The effect is real and semantic, but roughly one flip
in seven is noise, and on English roughly two thirds of the headline number is
suggestibility.

\label{sec:parser}

\label{tab:modes}

The released implementation ends its prompt at \texttt{\#\#\# Response:} without
stating an output format, then searches the continuation for
\texttt{[choice]\ldots@}. On XCOPA-vi this discards $45$--$47\%$ of predictions
as unparseable, at the same rate for Phi-2 and Qwen3.5-4B---a prompt--parser
mismatch, not a target failure. Where a prediction does survive, the parser
disagrees with a normalised argmax on $59\%$ of $150$ audited instances.

Table~\ref{tab:modes} shows what changes when $f$ is read from probabilities.
The gain is significant, but the informative comparison is
\textsc{prob\_accuracy} against \textsc{logprob}: $p=0.103$ at $n=500$, and
since $500$ is the entire test split this is a ceiling rather than a sample-size
limitation. \emph{Most of the improvement comes from abandoning the text parser,
not from the continuous signal.} Reporting the headline gain without this
decomposition would attribute it to the wrong change.

Table~\ref{tab:targets} makes the ordering plain: the target model moves
accuracy by $80$ points, far more than any effect the fidelity metric reports.
Phi-2 at $11.8\%$ on a two-choice task is \emph{below chance}
(95\% CI $[9.3, 14.9]$; restricted to the $273$ parseable answers, $21.6\%$,
CI $[17.1, 26.9]$), so it is selecting the wrong option systematically rather
than guessing. Control A collapses in step ($0.554 \to 0.122$), which is
coherent: a model that cannot read the question cannot read the hint either.

Splitting by whether the target answered at all reverses the aggregate picture.
Among instances with a correct prediction the corrected effect is
\emph{larger} in Vietnamese ($+0.475$, $n=59$) than in English ($+0.401$,
$n=354$); the unparseable instances contribute $+0.044$ and drag the mean down.
The fidelity measure survives the language change; the reader does not.

Swapping the explainer across four laboratories with everything else fixed
moves fidelity only between $0.930$ and $0.975$, so the metric is far more
sensitive to how predictions are read than to who writes the explanation.

\subsection{Cost scales inversely with success}

Vietnamese required $7.82$ optimisation iterations per instance against $3.11$
for English. Early stopping fires on the first non-zero score, so when the
measurement yields nothing every instance runs to the cap. The framework spends
the most compute exactly where it works least---a sharper form of the
computational cost the original raises in its own limitations.

\section{Error Analysis}

Of $14$ errors across both English runs, $13$ are parsing failures and one is a
genuinely wrong choice. What the pipeline calls accuracy is closer to a
parse-success rate.

The failure cascades. When the target's answer is unresolvable the pipeline
passes the literal string \texttt{X} to the explainer, which duly explains it:
\emph{``The model chose answer X based on the content of the question\ldots''}
The explanation, the contrary hint derived from it, and the resulting score are
then all about an answer that was never produced. This is why unparseable
instances show a corrected effect of only $+0.018$--$+0.044$: there is nothing
for an explanation to be faithful to, and the control correctly detects it.

Inspecting raw generations shows Phi-2 answering the plain prompt correctly in
English but, once \texttt{Let's think step by step.} is appended for scoring,
producing textbook-style exposition (``\emph{Use Case 1: Shipping Fragile
Items}'') that never states a choice. In Vietnamese it degenerates further,
emitting corrupted tokens (``\emph{Lê nhiêu}'', ``\emph{Bệnh hồi}'') in place of
the scaffold words. Both behaviours are invisible to a metric that reads only a
parsed answer.

\section{Limitations and Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Single repetition.} The original averages three runs; ours are single
runs, so we report no variance. This is the same gap we identify in the original,
and we flag it rather than repeat it silently.

\paragraph{Missing second metric and baselines.} We do not report truthfulness,
which requires gold explanations that COPA and XCOPA lack; ECQA
\citep{aggarwal2021ecqa} would be needed. The original's baselines
\citep{madsen2024selfexp,wang2022selfconsistency} are absent from the released
code and we did not reimplement them, so our numbers compare against the
original's published values rather than against re-run baselines.

\paragraph{Granularity.} Under the released mode $S_E$ carries one bit per
instance, since accuracy over a single example is $0$ or $1$. Small samples are
uninformative: a $30$-instance estimate of Vietnamese accuracy gave $33.3\%$ with
an interval that still contained chance, against $11.8\%$ at $n=500$. We twice
drew conclusions from $n=2$ that the full runs overturned.

\paragraph{Ethics.} All datasets are public research corpora. Explainer calls
were served through an institutional gateway; a small number of instances
containing violent lexical items returned empty completions from content
filtering, which we count and disclose rather than silently score as wrong. No
human subjects were involved.

\section{Conclusion}

\textsc{FaithLM} reproduces. Its contrary-hint score, however, mixes two things
that the original does not separate: whether the explanation carried the target's
reason, and whether the target follows hints at all. Two independently designed
controls agree that the second accounts for a large share of the reported number.
Under language transfer the binding constraint is not the metric but the
regular-expression reader in front of it, and reading probabilities---as the
original's own formulation specifies---recovers a significantly larger effect.
We recommend that intervention-based faithfulness measures report a null-hint
baseline alongside the headline score, and that the quantity being intervened on
be read from the model's distribution rather than from its prose.
